Science Technology

Sales up. Profits up. Where’s the cash?

Science Technology planned rapid growth while improving efficiency. I connected its income forecast to receivables, inventory, and financing needs, showing why a shorter cash cycle could still leave the company needing substantially more working capital.

Abstract

I assessed a plan to accelerate sales growth to 30% from a historical rate near 12%. The forecast improves the cash conversion cycle but still increases working capital from $147 million to $425 million. The central result is that efficiency gains can coexist with rising financing needs when the business expands quickly.

Growth consumes cash before it produces it

Receivables and inventory scale with the business. Faster turnover reduces cash tied up per dollar of sales, but a sufficiently large sales increase can still raise total working capital.

Test the plan’s operational assumptions

The case forecast reduces the cash conversion cycle from 375 days in 1984 to approximately 281 in 1989. Those improvements are assumptions in the plan, not achievements already observed.

Evaluate financing alongside profitability

Projected income rises while the cash forecast becomes more strained. Inventory execution and reliance on short-term borrowing are therefore central to whether the growth plan is credible.
