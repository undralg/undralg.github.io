Would cheaper e-bikes be enough?

Our team began with a rental subsidy proposal. A pretested street survey surfaced a broader question: what good is a cheaper ride if people cannot find a bike, or do not know the service exists?

Team NS51 course project · My work spanned questionnaire design, recruitment, cleaning, analysis, and writing · April 2023

Survey researchQuestionnaire designGoogle FormsData cleaningDescriptive statistics

Abstract

Would lower rental prices encourage people to use e-bikes? Our team combined desk research with a Google Forms street survey in San Francisco to explore that question. We pretested the questionnaire with 20 students, collected 35 field responses, and retained 30 residents after screening. Cost received the most selections among the listed barriers to trying e-bikes, but availability and awareness also appeared. The useful result was a broader set of adoption barriers to investigate, rather than a validated subsidy price. This retrospective rewrite separates the original findings from improvements identified on review.

Start with the decision, then question the proposal

The project began with San Francisco’s climate goals and a proposal to subsidize e-bike rentals. Our desk research considered local transport initiatives, rental pricing, and examples of support elsewhere. The survey added a different kind of evidence: how respondents traveled, how often they used e-bikes, and what discouraged them from trying one. A subsidy was our starting idea, not an established answer. Historical policy and pricing references describe the 2023 assignment, not today’s market.

Pretest, recruit, and screen

We tested a draft questionnaire with 20 Minerva students and revised ambiguous wording before fieldwork. The Google Forms questionnaire included consent, travel habits, usage frequency, demographic questions, and an option to write in other barriers. We recruited people near Trader Joe’s in two districts. Five of 35 responses came from nonresidents and were removed, leaving 15 residents per district. Selecting districts randomly did not make the people recruited on the street a random sample. The pretest participants were separate from the field sample.

Cost led, but it was not the only obstacle

Seventeen of the 30 residents reported using e-bikes extremely rarely to never. In the barrier chart, “too expensive” received 13 selections, lack of availability received seven, and lack of awareness received four. Other answers included safety, lack of information, and not needing a bike. These counts suggest that a price-only proposal could overlook practical obstacles. They do not tell us how many additional trips a discount would generate, or whether the same obstacles matter equally across the city.

Check the denominator before trusting the percentage

Reviewing the report exposed a reporting problem: the barrier figure refers to 26 responses, while the narrative gives a percentage that does not reconcile with that denominator. Respondents could select multiple answers, and the question was intended for people who had never tried e-bikes. Without the respondent-level export, I cannot verify which participants answered it. The chart therefore reports recorded selections rather than a percentage of residents. Missing answers, ineligible answers, and “no barrier” should not be treated as interchangeable.

A stated intention is not a measured price effect

The questionnaire also asked how usage would change under a substantial proposed price reduction. That is a hypothetical response, not observed purchasing behavior. The wording supplies a price anchor and asks about one discount range, so it cannot identify an optimal subsidy or a demand curve. A follow-up study could vary the price offer and measure actual redemption and completed trips, while recording bike availability. That would help separate price sensitivity from access constraints.

Who the sample leaves out

Seventeen of 30 respondents were aged 18 to 25, and four did not answer the income question. Recruitment location, willingness to participate, and the small sample restrict generalization. The data do not support a citywide prevalence estimate, a causal emissions claim, or a confident comparison between income groups. A stronger next round would screen residency before the questionnaire, recruit across more locations and times, and verify question eligibility and missingness in the raw export.

The recommendation changed in scope

The survey supports investigating affordability alongside availability and awareness. Before recommending a citywide subsidy, I would use a broader survey to refine the barriers and a small behavioral pilot to test whether an offer changes actual usage. The lesson is useful beyond transport: asking customers about price is only part of understanding why they do not adopt a product. Sometimes the first problem is finding it!
