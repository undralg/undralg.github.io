Would cheaper e-bikes be enough?

A survey of barriers to e-bike adoption in San Francisco

Survey researchQuestionnaire designGoogle FormsData cleaningDescriptive statistics

Abstract

Lower prices might encourage more people to use e-bikes, but affordability is only one part of the decision. We combined desk research with a survey of 30 San Francisco residents to explore transportation habits and barriers to e-bike use. Cost was the most frequently selected barrier, followed by availability and awareness. The findings suggest that a rental subsidy would be worth investigating alongside improvements in access and information. The small convenience sample provides exploratory evidence, rather than estimates representative of all San Francisco residents.

Start with the rider, not just the price

Our initial proposal was straightforward: make e-bike rentals more affordable to encourage their use. San Francisco’s hills make electric assistance appealing, and shared rentals offer access without purchasing a bike.

But before recommending a subsidy, we needed to understand what discouraged people from riding. Was the problem price, difficulty finding a bike, unfamiliarity with the service, or something else?

We reviewed local transportation initiatives and examples of bicycle subsidies elsewhere, then designed a survey to examine residents’ travel habits and reported barriers.

How we conducted the survey

We developed a Google Forms questionnaire covering transportation choices, cycling frequency, e-bike use, barriers, and demographics. Closed questions offered consistent response categories, while an “other” option allowed respondents to describe obstacles we had not anticipated.

We pretested the questionnaire with 20 Minerva University students and revised ambiguous wording before fieldwork. These participants were separate from the final sample.

Given the project’s time and resource constraints, we used street-intercept convenience sampling. We randomly selected two San Francisco districts, Districts 2 and 7, and approached people on the streets. Participation was voluntary, and the questionnaire included a consent statement and collected anonymous responses.

We collected 35 responses and excluded five from people who did not live in San Francisco. The analysis therefore covers 30 residents, with 15 from each district.

Randomly choosing districts broadened the locations included, but it did not make the respondents a random sample of the city. Recruitment still depended on who was present and willing to participate.

Cost mattered, but access mattered too

Seventeen of the 30 respondents reported using e-bikes “extremely rarely to never.”

A separate question asked people who had never tried e-bikes what discouraged them. Respondents could select multiple reasons.

Reported barrier Number of selections
Too expensive 13
Lack of availability 7
Did not know the service existed 4

Six other categories received one selection each. Counts represent selections, and multiple answers were permitted.

Cost received the most selections, supporting affordability as a useful focus for further research. However, availability and awareness also appeared. A cheaper rental offers little benefit to someone who cannot find a bike nearby or does not know the service exists.

These barriers could overlap. Someone might consider rentals expensive and difficult to access, meaning a price reduction alone may not resolve their hesitation.

What the sample can tell us

This survey identifies obstacles worth investigating. It cannot establish how common those obstacles are across San Francisco.

Seventeen of the 30 respondents were aged 18 to 25. Recruitment near grocery stores in two districts may underrepresent residents with different routines, travel needs, and access to transportation. Four respondents did not answer the income question, further limiting comparisons across income groups.

The questionnaire also asked how respondents expected their usage to change under a proposed price reduction. Such answers describe intentions, rather than observed behavior. They cannot establish how many additional trips a subsidy would generate or determine the most effective subsidy amount.

The findings are therefore most useful for refining a proposal and designing a stronger follow-up study.

From a cheaper ride to an easier choice

The results broadened our initial focus on rental prices. Affordability remained relevant, but availability and awareness also deserved attention.

A practical next step would be a small pilot that tracks actual rides following a discount offer, alongside bike availability and exposure to information about the service. This would help distinguish people who respond to a lower price from those whose main obstacle is access or unfamiliarity.

For a broader survey, we would recruit across more neighborhoods, locations, and times, screen for residency at the start, and separate questions for current users from those who have never tried an e-bike.

The central implication is simple: making e-bikes cheaper may help, but making them an easier choice requires understanding more than price.

Looking back: a cheaper ride, and more places to find one

October 2026 follow-up

The bikes became a much bigger part of the city. San Francisco recorded approximately 3.9 million Bay Wheels trips in 2025, compared with 2.33 million in 2023, an increase of roughly 67%. These totals include both electric and conventional bikes, so they measure growth in the shared-bike system rather than e-bikes alone. SFMTA, March 2026 update, p. 25

The developments after our survey addressed two of the barriers respondents identified. In November 2023, MTC and Lyft announced more than 2,000 additional e-bikes and 55 additional docking stations across the regional network. At the same time, annual membership fell from $169 to $150, and member e-bike charges fell from 20 to 15 cents per minute. Affordability and availability were being addressed together. MTC announcement, November 2023

Prices subsequently rose again, but the comparison depends on the rider. As of October 2026, the standard annual plan costs $165, with member e-bike rides at 17 cents per minute. Both exceed the November 2023 discounted rates, but remain below the rates immediately before that reduction. Lyft Bike pricing

For eligible riders, Bikeshare for All offers a different proposition: $5 for the first year, followed by $5 per month, with free unlocks and e-bike rides at five cents per minute, capped at $1 in usage charges. I later joined for $5 for my first year. Researching affordability is one thing; finding yourself using the discount is quite another! Bikeshare for All

The discounted program dates back to 2017, so its existence was not a later confirmation of our proposal. It also sharpens the awareness question: alongside asking whether riding is too expensive, a follow-up survey could ask whether eligible residents know about the reduced fares. MTC, September 2017

What held up was our focus on both affordability and access. Later expansion addressed both, and ridership grew. That sequence is consistent with our interpretation, although it does not separate the effects of lower prices, more bikes, more stations, or other changes. The stronger lesson is to investigate the price people actually face and the service they can actually reach.
